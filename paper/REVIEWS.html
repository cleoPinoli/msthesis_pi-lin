REVIEWER 1

This paper presents a formalisation in Agda of the Linear Calculus of Continuations (LCC). The principal contribution of this work is to show that relying on linear continuations to implement the state-like behaviour of session types yields several simplifications in the formalisation of the metaproperties of the language. The main design choices of the formalisation are as follows. - As mentioned above, exploiting linear continuations instead of working with native sessions. Technically, this intuitively means that at each interaction, a new continuation is pushed, instead of having to update the type of the channel where interaction takes place. This simplifies greatly the handling of typing contexts along the interactions between processes. - Working with an intrinsically-typed representation of processes. As a consequence of that, when formalising the calculus, Section 2.4 is somehow baked into sections 2.2 and 2.3, so that untyped processes are not considered at all. In turn, this representation is exploited to represent Pre-Congruence and Reduction, which involves some technicalities related to the manipulation of (nameless) channels and of the context. A good illustration of how things are both clever and a little intricate at the same time is given by the explanations of the s-wait and s-fork rules (pages 18-19). - The process calculus does not have a primitive notion of parallel composition, but has instead cut, that combines parallel composition and restriction.

As a result of these design choices, the proofs of several properties of typed processes turn out to be simple, or even straightforward in some cases. The cost of this approach is in terms of readability: the representation of the calculus departs in several aspects from how things would be phrased naturally when ignoring the task of formalisation. Nevertheless, the authors make a very good effort in explaining the formalisation and its intricacies. The paper is well organised and clear, which makes it very pleasant to read.

In the concluding sections of the paper, the authors compare their contribution with existing formalisations of sessions, and show what can be learnt from this effort. One notable observation is that the handling of context splitting, which has been the subject of several investigations, does not seem to cause much problems here. Indeed, the solution described in Section 3.2 is rather natural, and apparently does not give rise to complications.

I am in favor of acceptance of this paper.

Below are some suggestions for improvement, that can be taken into account before publication.

This contribution fits well in the landscape of formalisations of session types, and is certainly interesting in the context of the Concurrent Calculi Formalisation Benchmark. Beyond that, do you think that the approach behind this formalisation can lead to the development of generic libraries for formal proof, that could be used to formalise various calculi, including session types and the linearly typed pi-calculus?

DONE. We think that our formalisation is really tailored to LCC, we don't see substantial parts of the formalisation that could be turned into a library of general interest. In fact this is the reason why we have decided to provide separate figures in Table 5 for those formalisations that include a reusable library. We have expanded the concluding section, before we describe how the formalisation has been (or can be) extended, with some of these considerations.

Along the lines of the previous question, one can remark that like in other works in the field, you adopt a cut rule (739), instead of treating parallel composition independently from restriction.

This fits well with other design choices made in this work. Could this formalisation be adapted to handle parallel composition, thus allowing a more relaxed form of computation (developing further what you write in 1409-1412)?

DONE. We have added a discussion concerning unrestricted parallel composition (and some of the difficulties it would entail) in the concluding section, where we outline some future developments.

The section about related works should be enriched by discussing existing theorem prover formalisations of linear logic, independently from the link with session types. It could indeed be the case that some technical aspects related to the handling of the typing environment/of hypotheses and of channels/quantifiers are used in both contexts. Another line of works worth discussing is Actris/LinearActris, and more broadly Iris formalisations. To what extend does a proof environment based on separation logic bear some resemblances to the technical choices you make in this paper?

DONE. We have expanded the final part of the related work section with references to formalisations of linear logic and implementations of separation logic. We are not sure to fully understand the last question in the reviewer's report, since we feel that Actris and LinearActris have a weaker relationship with the concurrency benchmark. If the reviewer thinks that comparison with these frameworks needs to be detailed further, we will be happy to further revise this part of the section.

REVIEWER 2

A quick last minute note: I ended up writing quite a lot, but to me the only fix I believed important enough to mark this submission as "needs revision" is the paragraph that ends in "but I do think some equivalence result, stated as a theorem, would be very helpful." I think as long as that is addressed, all my other comments nice-to-haves and nothing mandatory nor grave.

This paper formalizes an alternative process calculus system, LCC. It supports all the connectives in Wadler's CP (the "basic" linear logic constructors + first order quantifications + bang). The authors prove subject reduction + deadlock freedom and mechanize the system + its proofs in Agda via an intrinsic typing encoding with co de Bruijn indices.

The paper is generally well-written, and the material is presented in a mostly gentle manner that makes the high-level details accessible to non-experts.

The stated contributions/novelties seem to be:

  1. Proposing a new calculus that is better suited for mechanization than CP based on the idea of making continuation channels explicit

  2. A mechanization of the system + subject reduction + deadlock freedom

Overall, the second contribution seems to support the claim given in the first contribution, given that, according to the authors, the mechanization is the most complete (most features) and most streamlined (some metric about code size being short). But also, the second is a strong contribution in of itself given that most other process calculus mechanizations omit the deadlock freedom proof.

Since I don't know Agda, I cannot confidently judge the technical details of section 3 (Agda Formalisation), but the code that was shown and its discussions seemed to make sense, at least from a total Agda newbie's perspective. I didn't quite understand the reason for fine-grained classification of threads/stuck processes/poised processes in section 3.7, but I'll defer to the authors (or other reviewers) on whether the section as written would be satisfactory for readers familiar with Agda.

COMMENT: we have expanded the paragraph right before the definition of the CanonicalCut data type to emphasize that the classiciation of processes helps reducing the number of cases that must be considered in defining a cut as canonical.

Regarding the contributions:

  1. I found the second contribution to be extremely convincing, because the mechanization does seem very elegant and also covers the entire system, as opposed to most mechanizations of session types that only covers a subset of Wadler's connectives + does not cover deadlock freedom. The related works give a fantastic overview of the literature on mechanization of session-typed systems.

My only quip is that authors use file size (after truncation of spaces/comments) as their attempt to get some understanding of "complexity" of mechanization. Of course, this is not a literature review paper on mechanizations, and most other papers use metrics such as lines of code, which is just as imperfect (actually, lines of code might be worse as argued by the authors), so it will be unfair/hypocritical for me to judge this paper from lacking more in-depth comparisons given that it is not the main point of this paper.

  1. However, I found the first contribution to be less convincing (but still somewhat convincing) compared to the second contribution. I think part of it is that the idea of explicit continuations has been done with SCP, which would be the analog of LCC, in Sano OOPSLA'23, though it also included separation of linearity checks. They also cover more metatheory (such as "adequacy", i.e., the equivalence proof of CP and SCP). Thus, from the theoretical perspective, the first contribution, to me, is actually:

a) extending SCP (minus the linearity predicates) to range over all of CP, instead of just the simple connectives

b) framing the system as one that is suitable for mechanization in the broad sense, as opposed to Sano's SCP which was merely a technical device that they internally used

c) giving evidence that this system (or rather, the insight of adding explicit continuation bindings) is a good idea when it comes to mechanization

For me, a is a weak contribution, b is a fairly strong contribution, and c is somewhere in between. a) is weak because none of the missing connectives in SCP (bang + quantifiers + additive units) offer any new challenges to making continuations explicit (I think, but maybe this can be clarified if there was some complexity that I'm not seeing). b) is fairly strong because, while it is not technical, it is a new perspective in the mechanization space. c) is medium because their only metric is the file size post-truncation. I think there are many other factors to consider with regards to that metric, for example, whether duplication of data structure due to certain abstraction styles can matter.

Also, given that a central point of the first contribution claim is to propose LCC as an alternative formulation of CP that is better suited for mechanization, I'd have expected some explicit statement that discusses the relationship of LCC vs. CP. There are few parts in the paper that informally mention the translation between LCC processes and CP processes and the equivalence of them. The underlying linear logic structure in both systems make them seem, to me, truly equivalent modulo renaming.

So, is it the case that if P |- Delta in LCC, [P] |- Delta in CP and vice versa (for suitable process translation [P]). Assuming that is the case, I feel like it is important to state it, because at least the way I read it, it seemed like an "obviously desirable" property, and the fact that it is missing in the paper sends some mixed signals to me. I don't think the exact details of the translation[P] is that important (though it may be worth sketching if there is space), but I do think some equivalence result, stated as a theorem, would be very helpful.

COMMENT. Following these remarks we have changed the paper as follows:

  • we have added a new section (section 4 in the revised version of the paper) where we sketch the correspondence between LCC and CP as suggested by the reviewer;
  • in the introduction, we have provided more details about the role of continuations in encoding sessions and also in the original interpretation of linear logic proofs as processes;
  • we have expanded the paragraph concerning SCP in the related work section to pinpoint the different roles of continuations in LCC and SCP.
